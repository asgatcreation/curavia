Security and privacy

What follows is how the system is actually built, not a list of intentions. Each claim corresponds to code and to a test that would fail if it stopped being true.

One hospital cannot see another's records

Every row of hospital data carries the hospital it belongs to, and the database layer refuses to return rows at all unless a hospital is in scope — so a developer who forgets to filter gets a crash, not a silent leak. The scope is derived from the signed-in staff member's own membership; it is never taken from anything the browser sends, so a forged request changes nothing.

Beneath that sits PostgreSQL row-level security as a second barrier, and above it a test suite whose only job is to attempt cross-hospital access — over the API, over WebSockets and through background jobs. A leak is a failing build.

Staff see only what their job needs

Access is decided by four independent checks: the hospital's plan, the modules it has switched on, the staff member's role, and a rule about the specific record. A cashier sees bills and names but no diagnosis — not hidden on screen, but absent from the response. An accountant sees money and no charts at all. A nurse sees her own ward. Sensitive fields, such as HIV status and psychiatric notes, need a separate permission and are encrypted in the database with keys held outside it.

Emergency access exists, because nobody should be locked out of a dying patient's record. It requires a written reason, lasts a short time, notifies the medical director immediately, and is flagged permanently in the audit log.

Every look is recorded, not just every change

Opening a patient's chart writes an audit entry. This is the part that matters, because the common real-world abuse is a staff member reading a record they have no business reading. Each entry carries the previous entry's cryptographic hash, so a deleted or altered row breaks the chain and the system reports exactly where. The log cannot be updated or deleted by the application at all, and the chain's tip is notarised nightly. The hospital's auditor and medical director can read it; so can the hospital, about us.

Curavia cannot read your patients' records

By default our own staff see hospital names, plans and system health — no patient data. To help with a problem we must ask, and the hospital grants a named person access for a limited period with a stated reason. Everything done under that grant is written into the hospital's own audit log, where the hospital can see it. When the grant expires, access ends by itself.

Your data stays yours

A hospital can export everything it holds, at any time, and leave. A hospital that stops paying becomes read-only; its records are never deleted without notice and an export first.

Under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023

The hospital is the data controller and Curavia is the data processor. That split is set out in the Data Processing Agreement, together with retention settings, consent records, how patients exercise access, export and deletion rights, and the process for notifying the hospital and the NDPC within 72 hours of a breach.

What this is not. Curavia is not a certified medical device. Its clinical decision support — interaction, allergy, dose and duplicate-therapy checks — is informational and does not replace clinical judgement. The public demonstration contains synthetic data only; no real patient information is used anywhere in it.
